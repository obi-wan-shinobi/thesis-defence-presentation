\begin{frame}
	\note<1->{
		Spectral bias is the tendency of neural networks trained with gradient descent to learn low-frequency structure before high-frequency detail.

		In other words, the network first captures the smooth, global shape of the target, and only later fits the more oscillatory parts.

		That is what these examples are meant to show. Even though the architectures are different, the learning process has the same qualitative pattern: low-frequency modes appear early, high-frequency modes are delayed.

		My thesis asks whether we can explain this from the training dynamics.

		The main idea is to reformulate spectral bias as an operator eigenvalue problem. If an operator governs the residual dynamics, then its eigenvalues determine which frequencies are learned quickly and which ones are learned slowly.

		So the three guiding questions are: what controls mode-wise learning rates, does the explanation survive finite data, and does it survive finite width?
	}

\begin{frame}
	\note<1->{
		Picture the error as a landscape: high where the network is wrong, low
		where it fits. Training rolls downhill, taking the steepest way down, which
		is why it is called gradient descent. Notice the steps shrink as it nears
		the bottom: the closer the error gets to zero, the smaller the correction,
		because the slope itself is proportional to how much error is left. Then we
		look at the same bowl from straight above. The rings are contours, lines of
		equal height, so closely spaced rings mean steep and widely spaced rings
		mean shallow. This bowl has a steep direction and a shallow one, and that is
		the distinction the next slide builds on.
	}

% Keystone slide: "rate = eigenvalue", introduced geometrically before any

\begin{frame}
	\note<1->{
		One sentence on what training does: it makes the error small. It does this by
		walking downhill on the error, stepping in the direction the error drops
		fastest, which is the negative gradient, hence gradient descent. Now the
		question this slide answers: why do different directions get learned at
		different speeds. Picture the error landscape as a stretched bowl. In the
		bowl's own tilted coordinates the descent path mixes the two directions
		together, so you cannot read off a clean rate for either one. The fix is to
		use the bowl's own axes as coordinates. These special directions, the
		eigenvectors, are the ones the dynamics never mixes. Along a single one of
		them the error falls in proportion to how much is left, the differential
		equation here, which is the same law as a cooling cup of coffee or
		radioactive half-life, so it decays exponentially, alpha of t equals alpha of
		zero times e to the minus lambda t. The rate lambda is the eigenvalue, large
		is fast, small is slow. Everything after this works out what these directions
		and rates are for a real network on the circle.
	}
	\vspace{0.4em}
	\slidetitle{}{Each direction has its own rate}
	{\textcolor{muted}{Training makes the error small by walking downhill, along the steepest drop.}}\par
	{\raggedleft\tiny\textcolor{muted}{recall the error: $\tfrac1n\sum_i\big(f(x_i)-f^\star(x_i)\big)^2$}\par}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
% Bridge 1: the scalar rate becomes an operator (the NTK). Answers the keystone's
% closing question in principle; stays model-agnostic (no f_m yet, that is SETUP).
\begin{frame}
	\note<1->{
		Picking up the question from the last slide. For a single direction the error
		obeyed alpha dot equals minus lambda alpha, the rate was one number. For a
		network the error is a whole function, the residual, and it still falls in
		proportion to how much is left, but the proportion is no longer a number. It
		is an operator acting on the whole function, and that operator is the neural
		tangent kernel. The residual obeys r dot equals minus T r. This holds for any
		differentiable model under gradient flow, which is why I am not writing the
		network down yet. If T were a fixed operator, diagonalizing it would split
		this back into one copy of the scalar law per eigenfunction, alpha k dot
		equals minus lambda k alpha k. So the eigenfunctions are the directions and
		the eigenvalues are the rates, and the question from the last slide becomes:
		what is the spectrum of T.
	}
	\vspace{0.4em}
	\slidetitle{}{The rate becomes an operator: the tangent kernel}
	{\textcolor{muted}{The same law as before, with the single rate replaced by an operator.}}

	\vspace{2mm}

\begin{frame}
	\note<1->{
		This is the main finding. At small width the kernel does move, and the move
		helps, but not in the way the frozen theory would predict. Two things happen
		together. First, alignment: the eigenspaces do not line up with the Fourier
		modes any better than at initialization, and at higher frequencies they line
		up worse. Second, strength: the operator becomes much stronger on the lowest
		frequency blocks, mostly k equal to zero and one. It is this added
		low-frequency strength, not better alignment, that tracks the lower loss. So
		gradient descent does not just learn low frequencies faster under a fixed
		spectrum, it reshapes the kernel to favour them more, a second-order version
		of the same bias. Two honesty points are on the slide: this is a correlation
		with lower loss rather than an established cause, and the whole effect is
		confined to small width, since at large width the frozen picture wins, the
		m equals 1024 reversal, exactly what lazy training predicts.
		Q\&A note: why the gain favours k=0 and k=1 is open. A likely cause is the
		parameterisation, since the constant and frequency-one parts sit directly in
		the pre-activation while higher harmonics only appear once ReLU acts. The
		clean test, not yet run, is a target with no constant or frequency-one
		component; if the gain persists there, the parameterisation account is wrong.

		\end{column}
		\begin{column}{0.32\textwidth}
			\begin{greencard}[height=40mm]
				\questionnum{green}{2}\ {\bfseries Finite data}\par\vspace{1.5mm}
				{\scriptsize Finite samples and frozen finite width preserve the picture, with explicit $O(n^{-1/2})$ and $O(m^{-1/2})$ control.}
			\end{greencard}
		\end{column}
		\begin{column}{0.32\textwidth}
			\begin{orangecard}[height=40mm]
				\questionnum{orange}{3}\ {\bfseries Finite width}\par\vspace{1.5mm}
				{\scriptsize Training evolves the kernel and reinforces the low-frequency bias. The frozen description holds where the kernel stays lazy, at large width.}
			\end{orangecard}
		\end{column}
	\end{columns}
\end{frame}

			\begin{greencard}[height=42mm]
				{\textcolor{green}{\bfseries Special}}\par\vspace{1.5mm}
				{\scriptsize Uniform measure on $S^1$ makes the NTK a convolution, so its eigenfunctions are exactly the Fourier modes.}
			\end{greencard}
		\end{column}
		\begin{column}{0.32\textwidth}
			\begin{bluecard}[height=42mm]
				{\textcolor{blue}{\bfseries Carries over}}\par\vspace{1.5mm}
				{\scriptsize Off this setting (non-uniform density, or Gaussian inputs giving Hermite polynomials) the basis changes, but eigenvalue size still sets the rates.}

			\begin{orangecard}[height=42mm]
				{\textcolor{orange}{\bfseries Open problem}}\par\vspace{1.5mm}
				{\scriptsize A non-asymptotic theory for the evolving kernel: control of $T_t^{(m)}-T_0^{(m)}$ over training time.}
			\end{orangecard}
		\end{column}
	\end{columns}
